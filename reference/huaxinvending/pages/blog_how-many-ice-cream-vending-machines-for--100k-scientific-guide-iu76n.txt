How Many Ice Cream Vending Machines Do You Need to Make $100K a Year?
How Many Ice Cream Vending Machines Do You Need to Make $100K a Year?
Date：2026-10-27 Author：Huaxin

Machines needed = $100K ÷ (daily cups × contribution per cup × operating days − fixed cost per machine). Three worked cases and how many sites you need

To work out how many vending machines you need to make $100K a year, divide the target by what one machine earns after its own costs:

Machines needed = $100,000 ÷ [(average daily cups × contribution per cup × operating days) − annual fixed cost per machine]

With the mid-range assumptions used later in this guide, the answer is six ice cream vending machines. With weaker sites the same formula gives 13, and with strong sites, three. The machine is identical in all three cases. What moves the count is how many cups each location sells and what it costs to occupy, so in practice the question becomes how many locations you can secure that reach a minimum daily sales figure. Section 4 shows how to set that minimum.

This guide is for first-time operators and investors asking how many ice cream vending machines they need for an income target. It explains each input, works through three illustrative cases, and shows how many cups a day each site must average for a given fleet size. Payback on the purchase and a full profit model are covered in our ROI calculation guide. Huaxin manufactures fully automatic ice cream vending machines. Every figure below is an assumption chosen to demonstrate the method, not a customer result or a forecast.

1 The formula and what goes into each input

The bracket in the formula is the yearly income per machine: what one machine earns at its site after the costs that come with that site. The method works for any vending machine income target. What is specific to ice cream is the cost per cup and the number of service visits.

Three points decide whether the result can be used. Put rent in the right line: a fixed monthly rent belongs in fixed cost, while a percentage of sales reduces the contribution per cup, and the two behave differently in a slow month. Round up: 5.1 machines means six machines, or five sites that sell more. Check the sign of the bracket: if one machine's yearly income is zero or negative, adding more machines of that kind moves you further from the target.

Average daily cups has to be a full-year figure. In the United States, factory production of regular hard ice cream in July 2025 was around 15 percent above that year's monthly average, and December's was around 18 percent below it [1]. Factory output is not vending sales, but it shows how far demand for the product moves through the year. A daily figure from a July week, multiplied across the year, inflates the result. Either model the slow months separately or use the annual average.

Machine capacity is a separate question. B Series machines are rated at up to 120 cups an hour under standard conditions. That rating tells you whether a machine can keep up with a busy hour. It does not tell you how many cups a site will sell in a day, which is the figure the formula needs.

Monthly fixed costs: low case $1,000 (rent $400, service visits $400, electricity, data and maintenance $200); mid case $1,800 (rent $1,100, service visits $450, electricity, data and maintenance $250); high case $900 with no fixed rent (service visits $650, electricity, data and maintenance $250). Yearly income is before income tax and before recovering the machine purchase.

The high case needs only three machines, but it assumes three sites that each average 80 paid cups a day over 300 days. Sites like that are scarce, and the venues that offer them usually want a share of sales, which is why this case has the lowest contribution per cup. The share has one advantage: when sales drop in a slow month, the venue's cut drops with them, while a fixed rent stays the same.

The mid case shows why rounding up matters. Five machines would earn $98,550 a year, just under the target, so the plan needs a sixth machine or five sites that each sell a few more cups.

Service cost grows with sales. A B83 Max, B84, B85, B86 or B86 Max holds about 160 cups, so a machine selling 80 a day needs its cups restocked at least every second day, and mix, toppings and cleaning are usually handled on the same visits. Cost those visits at a real hourly rate even if you make them yourself. Otherwise part of your $100K is your own wage.

None of the three cases recovers the cost of the machines. If you want $100K a year after paying for the fleet, take each machine's landed cost (price, freight, import charges and installation), spread it over the years you expect to run the machine, and add the yearly amount to the fixed cost line.

Between 35 and 55 cups a day, the requirement falls from ten machines to four. Below about 24 cups a day under these costs, each machine loses money at its site, so adding machines of that kind takes the plan further from the target. Daily cups and site cost are both properties of the location, which is why the location decides the count.

A fleet count also assumes that every machine has an open site. An operator in Italy started two B84 machines in early 2026. In late February he told us one of them was switched off because the shopping center it stood in had closed, and the machine needed a new location. In the same exchange he said this was probably not the best time of year and that he expected sales to rise in summer. For that stretch, his two-machine plan was selling from one site, in the slow season.

Required daily cups per site = (annual target ÷ number of machines + annual fixed cost per machine) ÷ (contribution per cup × operating days)

A larger fleet lowers the bar for each site only a little, because every new site brings its own rent and service cost. What it changes more is risk. With four machines, one site that closes or fails takes away a quarter of the plan's income. With ten, it takes away a tenth, and the machine can move to the next site on your list while the other nine keep selling.

Agree terms with the sites before you fix the size of the order. Until a venue has agreed, the rent and the daily cups in your formula are estimates. A buyer in Chile asked us in June 2026 for the operating costs because he needed them to calculate the project's return, and in August he was still negotiating the lease for his shopping-mall site. He was renting floor space by the square meter, so before signing he asked how much clearance the machine needs at the sides and back, since that space would be added to his rent. Count the locations that have agreed, or are close to agreeing, and size the first order to that number.

5 From a machine count to a first order

Once the formula gives you a number, the purchase usually goes in stages. A single machine is the right first order when a site, the payment setup or your refill routine still has to be proven, and it replaces the assumed daily cups in this guide with a measured figure. For formal operation, Huaxin recommends planning around ten units: the machines ship together, share spare parts and written procedures, can be serviced on planned routes, and spread the risk of any one site, as section 4 shows. Our guide to choosing between a single-unit pilot and a 10-unit rollout explains what to measure during a pilot and how to move from one machine to ten.

For a phased purchasing suggestion, send us the number and types of locations you have lined up through our inquiry form, for example three mall sites and two family entertainment centers, with your country and when each site can open. We will suggest how to split the order into batches: which sites to start with, the configuration for each, and when to place the next order.
