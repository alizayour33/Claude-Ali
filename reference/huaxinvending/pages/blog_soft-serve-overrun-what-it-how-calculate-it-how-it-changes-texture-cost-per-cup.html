Soft Serve Overrun: What It Is, How to Calculate It, and How It Changes Texture and Cost per Cup

Date：2026-11-11 Author：Huaxin

What soft serve overrun is, how to measure it with a cup and a scale, how it changes texture and mix cost per cup, and how Huaxin machines control it

Soft serve overrun is the percentage by which a mix grows in volume as air is whipped into it during freezing. If 1 liter of mix becomes 1.4 liters of soft serve, the overrun is 40%. Published figures for soft serve run from about 25% to 80%, depending on the recipe and the machine [4]. That one number decides two things: how the product eats, and how many cups a liter of mix will fill.

Huaxin manufactures fully automatic ice cream vending machines. This article is for operators, chain brands and dessert businesses with their own recipes who care about product quality and ingredient cost. It explains how to calculate soft serve overrun percentage, how overrun changes texture and the amount of mix in each cup, and how our machines control it. It does not cover recipe design or choosing a mix; for that, see our soft serve mix and ingredient guide.

What Is Soft Serve Overrun

In the freezing cylinder, the mix is frozen and whipped at the same time. Air is beaten in, so the finished product takes up more space than the mix it came from. That extra volume, expressed as a percentage of the mix volume, is the overrun [1]. At 100% overrun, 1 liter of mix makes 2 liters of ice cream, and half the finished volume is air [2].

Overrun has a practical ceiling. In the US, a product sold as ice cream must weigh at least 4.5 pounds per gallon [3], or about 540 grams per liter, which caps overrun at roughly 100% [2]. Soft ice cream overrun normally sits below that line.

How to Calculate Overrun

By volume, the formula is [1]:

Overrun (%) = (volume of soft serve − volume of mix used) ÷ volume of mix used × 100

A vending machine serves one cup at a time, so the finished volume is hard to measure. Weighing is easier on site: fill the same cup with mix and then with soft serve, and weigh each [1].

Overrun (%) = (weight of a cup of mix − weight of the same cup of soft serve) ÷ weight of the same cup of soft serve × 100

Here is how to do it:

  1. Use a cup with a fixed volume and a flat rim, such as a stainless steel measuring cup. Put it on a digital scale and tare it.

  2. Fill it with chilled liquid mix, level it off at the rim and weigh it. If you use a powder mix, reconstitute it to the recipe ratio first.

  3. Wash and dry the cup. Fill it with soft serve straight from the machine and level it off. Avoid air pockets, but do not press the soft serve down. Weigh it right away.

  4. Apply the formula. Weigh before adding sauce or toppings. They hold none of the whipped-in air and will skew the result [1].

Example (illustrative): a cup filled with mix weighs 220 g, and the same cup filled with soft serve weighs 150 g. Overrun = (220 − 150) ÷ 150 × 100 ≈ 47%.

A single weighing describes a single cup. Weigh one after start-up, one after a run of peak-hour sales and one just after a refill, then average them. That shows what this machine actually does with this mix.

What Makes Soft Serve Soft

Inside the freezing cylinder, soft serve and hard ice cream are in much the same state. The difference comes afterward. Hard ice cream is packed and sent for hardening, while soft serve goes straight into the cup with roughly half its water frozen [5]. That is why soft serve is served warmer, at about −6°C, compared with about −12°C for hard ice cream [6]. The recipes differ too: soft serve usually contains 3% to 10% milk fat [6].

Overrun plays a part, since ice cream with too little air is compact and hard [7]. But soft serve is soft mainly because of its serving temperature and its recipe. On our B Series machines, the firmness of the product is controlled by the hardness setting on the parameter screen.

How Overrun Changes Texture and Cost per Cup

Texture and Consistency

With too little overrun, soft serve is dense, hard and heavy. With too much, it has a weak body, feels foamy in the mouth and melts quickly [7]. No single number suits every mix. Change the recipe and the same overrun can eat quite differently. So when we assess a mix, we look at what it produces on the machine instead of starting from a target percentage.

For a vending machine, steady overrun matters more than a few points higher or lower. In our user manual, the first cause listed for uneven portion sizes is inconsistent aeration: when overrun drifts, the same dispense time gives cups of different sizes. The manual also covers mix that stays in the machine too long and is frozen again and again. Its overrun drops, the fat and water separate, and the soft serve turns icy.

Raising overrun from 30% to 40% cuts the mix in each cup by about 7%. The same arithmetic decides how many cups a full hopper makes. The B83 Max to B86 Max hold about 20 liters of mix, and the number of servings from one refill depends on serving weight, overrun, recipe and losses.

The table counts only the mix, though. Push overrun beyond what a mix can hold to save on ingredients, and the soft serve turns foamy and melts fast. Customers notice. The right target is the overrun at which your mix still tastes and holds up the way you want, and you only find it by running the mix on the machine.

How Huaxin Machines Control Overrun

A customer in Chile read our manual and asked: the parameter screen has no overrun setting, so can overrun be set to 30% to 35%, and how?

He was right about the screen. On our machines, overrun results from the feed system, the freezing settings and the mix itself. It is not a number you type in.

Gravity Feed

Our machines use gravity feed. On the B Series, mix flows from the refrigerated hopper into the 2-liter freezing cylinder under its own weight. Gravity-fed machines typically draw in air through an air tube. They make a denser soft serve, usually at about 25% to 40% overrun, and have fewer parts, which makes them easier to clean and maintain [4]. For more on the system, see our Cloud Puffing system.

Settings That Change What Lands in the Cup

The manual lists three settings that directly affect what goes into the cup:

  • Hardness: set in the main settings on the parameter screen. If it is set too high, the freezing cylinder cannot keep up, portions get smaller, and the mixing motor may trip its overload protection.

  • Dispense time: sets the size of each cup.

  • Hopper temperature: the temperature at which mix is held before it enters the freezing cylinder.

A new mix usually means adjusting these settings again. Send us details of your mix, and we will recommend settings based on a test on the machine.

Daily Conditions That Keep Overrun Stable

Freezing and aeration are automatic. What operators need to keep in place are a few daily conditions, all covered in the user manual:

  • Do not leave mix in the hopper too long. Mix that sits and is frozen repeatedly loses overrun and turns icy.

  • Keep foreign objects out of the hopper. They can block the feed line.

  • Leave clearance around the machine so heat can escape. Poor heat dissipation or low voltage keeps the cylinder from freezing properly, and the product changes with it.
