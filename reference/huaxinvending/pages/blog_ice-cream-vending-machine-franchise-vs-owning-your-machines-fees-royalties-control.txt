Ice Cream Vending Machine Franchise vs Owning Your Machines: Fees, Royalties and Control
Ice Cream Vending Machine Franchise vs Owning Your Machines: Fees, Royalties and Control
Date：2026-11-03 Author：Huaxin

Compare an ice cream vending machine franchise with owning your machines: upfront fees, royalties, territory, brand control, and when a franchise still fits.

If you are looking into an ice cream vending machine franchise, you probably have two options in front of you. You can pay a franchise fee and open under an established brand, or you can buy machines directly from a manufacturer and run them under your own name. The real difference is what your money buys. A franchise sells you a brand and a set of operating rules. In return you pay an upfront fee and a royalty on sales, and you accept limits on suppliers, territory and contract length. Owning your machines buys you the equipment itself. You decide the brand, the prices and the ingredient suppliers, and you also take on finding locations, building the brand and arranging maintenance.

Huaxin manufactures the ice cream vending machines discussed here. We sell machines and we don’t offer franchises, so this comparison is written from a manufacturer’s side, and it also sets out the cases where a franchise is the better fit. It is meant for investors, small operators and chain businesses that want to run their own brand. Fees and rules follow the US market; readers in Europe, Australia and the Middle East can check the same items against their local terms. For a full start-up budget, see our ice cream vending machine startup cost and investment planning guide.

What You Pay For in a Franchise

The US Federal Trade Commission’s guide to buying a franchise sets out the costs and controls plainly[1]. Whether the concept is an ice cream kiosk franchise or a frozen yogurt vending machine franchise, the fees come from the same few places.

Initial franchise fee. Paid once at signing for the right to use the brand and for opening support. The FTC says it typically ranges from tens of thousands of dollars to several hundred thousand dollars[1]. Equipment, site setup and opening supplies are usually extra.

Royalties. An ongoing payment based on a percentage of your weekly or monthly gross income[1], and the FTC points out that you usually owe it even when the business is losing money[1]. FRANdata, a franchise research firm, reviewed 2,425 franchise brands over 2012–2018 and found an average royalty of 5.9%, with frozen dessert concepts averaging 5.4%[2].

Advertising and other fees. Many systems also require contributions to an advertising fund[1]. In July 2024 the FTC said franchisees had reported ever-increasing payment processing and technology fees, and its staff guidance stated that franchisors cannot lawfully charge fees that were not disclosed in advance[3].

Approved suppliers. A franchisor can restrict what you sell and may require you to buy supplies only from approved suppliers[1].

Territory and term. An “exclusive” or “protected” territory may stop the franchisor and other franchisees from opening competing outlets near you, though it may not protect you from all competition by the franchisor[1]. Renewal is not automatic, and if the franchise is terminated you are likely to lose your entire investment[1].

Royalties are charged on sales, not profit. Taking the frozen dessert average above as an example, every $10,000 your machine takes in sends $540 to the franchisor before you pay for mix, cups, rent or card fees. (Illustrative calculation. The actual rate is set by each franchise agreement.)

Not Every “Vending Franchise” Is a Franchise

Many offers marketed as a vending machine franchise work differently. The seller takes a payment, supplies the machines and promises to find locations for you. In the US, an arrangement in which the seller says it will provide customers or locations may fall under the FTC’s Business Opportunity Rule. The seller then has to give you a one-page disclosure document at least seven days before you sign or pay, and any earnings claim must be backed by written proof[4]. A franchise, by contrast, must provide its Franchise Disclosure Document (FDD) at least 14 days before you sign, and any claims about sales, income or profits must appear in Item 19 of that document[1]. Whatever the seller calls the deal, ask for the matching document before you sign anything.

Ongoing costs and territory are where the two models differ most. Royalties rise with your revenue, so the more you sell, the more you pay. Owning your machines removes that cost, but nobody shields you from competitors in your city either. When you compare upfront investment, or vending machine franchise cost, set the full totals side by side instead of matching the franchise fee against the machine price. A franchise package may bill equipment and site setup separately, and when you buy direct you need to add branding design, freight and import costs yourself.

When a Franchise Is the Better Choice

We sell machines, but there are situations where a franchise may genuinely suit you better.

Your locations value a brand shoppers already know. Some malls, attractions and transit sites prefer well-known brands when they lease space. If the franchisor can show venue agreements that are already signed, that is real value. Ask to see them in writing before you commit.

You want protection from other units of the same brand. A franchise agreement can define a protected territory. Owning your machines does not come with one.

You want someone else to handle brand and marketing. Running your own brand means building the name, design, social media and promotion from nothing.

Owning is usually the more sensible route if you already have locations or foot traffic (a dessert shop, café, entertainment center or an existing vending route, for example), if you want your own brand, or if you plan to expand across several sites or resell machines. In those cases you already have much of what a franchise system would be selling you.

If you do choose a franchise, read the full FDD before signing. Look closely at the earnings claims in Item 19, the list of supplies you must buy from the franchisor, and who owns the machines when the agreement ends, including whether you can move or sell them. Then talk to a few current franchisees about how the system works in practice.

Territory Protection for Distributors and Multi-Site Operators

Territory is the question buyers raise most often. Usually they want to know two things: whether they can buy directly from the factory, and whether there is already an exclusive distributor in their country.

If you run your own machines, we don’t limit where you place them or how many you operate, and we don’t grant territory protection by default. Other operators in the same city can buy machines from us too. If your country already has a distributor with a signed agreement and regional protection, your inquiry may be handled by that distributor. For customers planning several locations, we usually recommend testing one machine at a real site first and then planning the rollout in batches of around 10 units. Volume pricing can be evaluated by model, configuration, customization and order size, and network-enabled machines can be managed together from the back office. See our wholesale plans for multi-site ice cream vending.

For distributors, territory protection and exclusivity are not default terms either. We discuss regional support with partners that have local sales, service and market development capabilities, and the terms depend on purchase plans, annual volume, after-sales capability and marketing investment. Regional protection may be discussed based on sales commitments and a market development plan, and it requires a signed cooperation agreement and approval from our sales management. We don’t offer unconditional exclusive distribution.

A European ice cream business that already runs two of our machines is a useful example. In September it wrote to ask for a degree of territory protection in its home country, meaning that “inquiries and potential customers from the [national] market would be handled through us rather than supplied directly.” It proposed discussing annual machine volumes, pricing, territory protection and exclusivity terms once its own testing results were in.

Our sales records show that many distribution partnerships start with the partner running its own machines. A customer in Germany watched our machines operating at a trade show, ordered one sample machine and tested it locally for more than three months, then bought a full container and later became our distributor. A customer in Spain first bought two of our machines, together with two from another manufacturer for comparison. After testing, the customer reordered several times and moved from placing machines itself to selling equipment.

FAQ
Is there a franchise fee when I buy Huaxin machines?

No. You pay for the machines and for any options you choose, such as a branded exterior or a payment configuration, as listed in the quotation. We charge no franchise fee and take no percentage of your sales.

Can I use my own brand on the machine?

Yes. The exterior wrap, logo, brand colors and graphics follow your brand guidelines, and the interface can display your logo, menu images and promotional videos. You pay us nothing for using your own brand. The minimum order and lead time for fully exclusive designs or deeper software changes are confirmed for each project.

If you want to plan around owning your machines, send us your project details with three pieces of information: your country, the number of locations you plan, and whether the machines will carry your own brand. We will reply with a recommended configuration and a plan for buying the machines outright.
